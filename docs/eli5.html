Explain it like I'm five

You test the fire alarm on purpose.
Do the same for your security.

You don't wait for a real fire to find out the smoke detector's battery is dead. You press the button, hear the beep, and sleep better. chaotic-shoebill-stork does that for the alarms protecting your software.

The idea

Alarms that are never tested are just decorations.

Every serious building has fire alarms, sprinklers, and exits. But the building that's actually safe is the one that runs fire drills — where someone deliberately sets off the alarm under safe, controlled conditions to check three things: Did the alarm go off? Did people hear it? Did help arrive in time?

Software has security "alarms" too. Firewalls. Logins that should lock the door when something looks wrong. Monitors that are supposed to shout when data walks out. The uncomfortable truth is that most of these are never actually tested after the day they're installed. They sit there, assumed to work, until the one night they're really needed — and sometimes they don't.

The shoebill is a tall, still stork that waits patiently by the water, then strikes with sudden precision. That's the idea here: a patient, careful tester that gently pokes your defenses to prove they'll strike back when it counts.
The difference

Two ways to find out if your alarm works.

Wait for a real burglar

  • You discover the alarm was unplugged while it's being robbed.
  • It's 3am, everything's on fire, and now you're debugging.
  • Or you wait a whole year and hope the annual audit catches it.
  • The damage is already done by the time you know.

Run a safe drill on purpose

  • You safely simulate the burglar — on a tiny, controlled scale.
  • You watch: did the lock hold? did the alarm sound? did a guard come?
  • You undo everything immediately and check it's back to normal.
  • You fix the gap on a calm Tuesday, not during a crisis.
A drill, step by step

What actually happens during one test.

It's the same careful routine every single time — and a strict safety checker gets the final say before anything happens at all.

Make a clear bet

"If the identity service goes down, logins should refuse — not quietly let everyone in." We write that down as a precise, checkable claim.

Ask the safety checker

A built-in gatekeeper runs through ten safety questions — is anything already on fire? is this a bad time? do we have permission? If any answer is wrong, the drill is politely refused.

Do one small, reversible thing

We create the controlled hiccup — nothing destructive, nothing permanent, and nothing involving real customer data. Just enough to see how the defense reacts.

Watch carefully

Did the control block it? Did something notice? Did an alert fire? Did a real person get paged — and fast enough? We record all four.

Put everything back

We immediately undo the hiccup and double-check the system is exactly as it was. If something couldn't be undone, the safety checker would never have allowed it.

Give an honest grade

Pass, fail, or "we couldn't tell" — never fudged. The result gets a tamper-proof signature, like a sealed inspection certificate, and goes on a report card for that service.

The honest part

It is built to never kid you.

"I don't know" is never "pass"

If the test couldn't actually observe what happened, it says so — it will not round that up to a passing grade.

An untested lock scores zero

If a door was supposed to be checked and never was, its score is 0 — because "we never looked" is not the same as "it's fine."

No exploits, no real data

This is not a hacking tool. It never breaks in for real and never touches real personal data — only harmless, trackable decoys.

Think of the result as a safety inspection report for your software's defenses — one you can actually trust, because every line is signed and every "we're not sure" is labelled honestly.
